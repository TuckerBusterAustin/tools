How to write an SEO audit report a client will actually read

The problem with most audit reports

Most SEO tools export dozens of pages of warnings. The client opens it, sees 140 issues in red, and either panics or closes it. Either way, nothing gets fixed. A report only helps if it gets read and acted on, so the goal is a short document that answers three questions:

1. What's wrong?

2. How much does it matter?

3. What should we do, and who does it?

Step 1: Decide the scope up front

Say exactly what you audited at the top: "On-page audit of 5 pages: home, services, pricing, contact, blog index. Checked on 16 October 2026." That stops the client assuming you looked at everything, and it gives you a clean baseline to compare against next time.

Step 2: Cut the findings down

Tools report everything they can measure, and much of it doesn't matter for this client. Before you write anything:

  • Merge duplicates. "Missing alt text" on 30 images is one finding, not 30.
  • Drop noise. If a warning wouldn't change what you'd advise, leave it out or move it to an appendix.
  • Group by fix, not by symptom. If one template bug causes a wrong canonical on every page, that's one item: "fix the template".

A good on-page report usually comes down to 5-15 items.

Step 3: Rank by severity

Give each item a level and sort by it. A simple three-level scale works well:

  • High: can stop the page from showing up properly in search, or makes it look broken. Examples: missing <title>, a canonical pointing at the wrong URL, no viewport tag on mobile.
  • Medium: hurts click-through or clarity. Examples: missing or generic meta description, no <h1>, key images without alt text.
  • Low: polish. Examples: vague link text, missing Open Graph image, extra H1s.

Pitfall: don't mark everything high. If it's all urgent, nothing is.

Step 4: Write each item in plain words

Use the same small template for every item:

> [Severity] What's wrong, in one sentence.

> Why it matters, in one sentence, with no jargon.

> The fix, specific enough to hand to a developer.

Example:

> [High] The services page tells Google that the homepage is the "real" version of it.

> Because of this, Google may leave the services page out of search results.

> Fix: change the canonical tag on /services to https://example.com/services. It's currently set to https://example.com/.

Compare that with a typical tool line: Canonical mismatch: non-self-referencing canonical detected. Both describe the same problem, but only the first one gets fixed.

Wording tips:

  • Explain what's broken by its effect ("may not appear in search"), not its tag name. Put the tag name in the fix line, where the developer needs it.
  • Give the exact current value and the exact target value whenever you can.
  • Leave out ranking promises. "Should help the page appear correctly" is honest. "Will boost you to page one" isn't something anyone can promise.

Step 5: Add owner and effort (optional but useful)

Two extra columns make a list easier to act on:

| Item | Severity | Who | Effort |

|---|---|---|---|

| Fix canonical on /services | High | Developer | 10 min |

| Write meta descriptions for 5 pages | Medium | Client / copywriter | 1 hr |

| Add alt text to product photos | Medium | Client | 30 min |

Effort estimates are guesses, so label them that way. Even so, they help the client see that most fixes are small.

Step 6: Keep the format simple

  • Email or doc first. For a small audit, a plain-text fix list pasted into an email often works better than an attachment, because it's read straight away.
  • A PDF for anything formal. Put your name, the client's name, the date and the scope on the first page. Clients forward reports, and a clean header makes clear who did the work.
  • One page of summary. If the report runs longer, open with a short "top 3 fixes" section.

Step 7: Plan a re-check

End with a short line like: "Once these are done, I'll re-run the audit to confirm." That makes the report the start of a process instead of a one-off, and the before-and-after comparison is easy proof of your work.

Common mistakes

  • Sending the raw tool export as the report.
  • Mixing on-page fixes with big strategy advice in one list. Keep them separate.
  • Leaving out the current value, so the developer has to rediscover the problem.
  • No date and no scope, so nobody knows later what was checked.
