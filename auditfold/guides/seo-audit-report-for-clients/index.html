How to write an SEO audit report a client will actually read

Most SEO audit reports fail in the same way: they are long, full of tool output, and the client can't tell what to do first. If you are a solo consultant or a small agency, the report is often the only thing the client sees of your work. It needs to read like advice, not like a log file.

Here is a structure that works for on-page audits, plus examples of rewriting findings so they make sense to a client.

Know who reads it

Usually there are two readers:

  • The owner or marketing lead, who wants to know "is this bad, and what will it cost me?"
  • Whoever makes the changes: a developer, a web designer, or the client in their CMS. They need exact instructions.

Write the top of the report for the first reader and the fix list for the second.

A structure that works

`

1. One-paragraph summary

2. Fix list, ranked (High / Medium / Low)

3. What's already fine

4. Next steps and who does what

`

That's the whole report. Put screenshots and raw tool output in an appendix, or leave them out.

1. Summary

Three or four sentences. What you checked, the overall state, and the one or two things that matter most.

> I checked the homepage and the three service pages. The basics are mostly in place, but two service pages share the same title and description, so search results can't tell them apart. Fixing that is the priority. Everything else is small.

2. Ranked fix list

Each item gets a severity, the page it's on, the problem in plain words, and the fix.

| Severity | Page | Problem | Fix |

|---|---|---|---|

| High | /services/roofing | The canonical tag points to the homepage, telling search engines this page is a copy of it | Change the canonical to https://example.com/services/roofing/ |

| Medium | /services/roofing | No meta description | Add a 1-2 sentence summary of the roofing service and service area |

| Low | /about | 4 images have no alt text | Add short descriptions, e.g. "Team photo outside the shop" |

How to set severity honestly:

  • High: it changes how search engines index or display the page. Examples: a missing or duplicated title, a wrong canonical, no mobile viewport tag.
  • Medium: it weakens the page but doesn't break anything. Examples: a missing description, no H1, missing share-preview (Open Graph) tags.
  • Low: polish. Examples: overlong titles, vague link text, heading levels out of order.

Resist marking everything High. If every item is urgent, the client will tune out all of them.

3. What's already fine

A short list of what passed. It shows you checked thoroughly, and it stops the client from "fixing" things that work.

4. Next steps

Who does each fix, and roughly when. If you're offering to do the work, put that here, once.

Rewriting findings in plain words

The biggest gain comes from translating. Some before-and-after examples:

  • "Missing meta description" becomes "Google has no summary written for this page, so it makes one up from the page text. Add one that says what you offer and where."
  • "Multiple H1 tags" becomes "The page has three main headlines. Pick one that says what the page is about and make the others subheadings."
  • "Missing viewport meta tag" becomes "On phones, this page loads zoomed out like a desktop site. One line of code fixes it."
  • "og:image not set" becomes "When someone shares this link, the preview has no picture."

Rules of thumb:

  • Say what the visitor or searcher experiences, then what to change.
  • Give the exact code or text when you can.
  • Avoid predicting traffic or rankings. You can't promise them, and clients remember.

Pitfalls

  • Pasting tool scores without context. "72/100" means nothing on its own. If you include a score, say what would move it.
  • Auditing a JavaScript-rendered page from raw source only. Tags may be added by scripts. Check the rendered page in DevTools before you report a tag as missing.
  • Mixing scopes. If the audit covers on-page only, say so at the top, so the client doesn't assume you checked backlinks or speed.
  • No re-check. Offer a short follow-up audit after the fixes. It confirms the work, and it is a natural next conversation.

Format and delivery

For a small client, a well-written email or a one- or two-page PDF beats a 40-page deck. Plain text survives forwarding, pasting into tickets and reading on a phone. Put your name and the client's name at the top, along with the date and the URLs checked.
