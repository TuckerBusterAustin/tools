How to check when an SSL certificate expires

The short answer

Every SSL/TLS certificate carries an expiry date, shown as Expires On or Not After. You can read it in a few seconds three ways:

1. In your browser, from the padlock or site-info icon.

2. On the command line, with openssl.

3. With an online checker that connects to the site for you.

It matters more than it used to. Under CA/Browser Forum ballot SC-081, the longest lifetime for a publicly trusted certificate fell to 200 days on 15 March 2026. It drops to 100 days in March 2027 and to 47 days in March 2029 (summary of the timeline). So renewals come round more often, and so do chances for one to fail.

Method 1: The browser padlock

This is the fastest way to check one site you can open.

Chrome / Edge

1. Open the site over https://.

2. Click the icon to the left of the address bar.

3. Choose Connection is secure, then Certificate is valid.

4. On the General tab, read Expires On.

Firefox

1. Click the padlock, then Connection secure, then More information.

2. Click View Certificate.

3. Under Validity, read Not After.

Safari (macOS)

1. Click the padlock in the address bar.

2. Click Show Certificate, then expand Details and find Expires.

Pitfall: the browser shows the certificate it received. If the site sits behind a CDN or proxy such as Cloudflare, that's the edge certificate. The certificate on your origin server can be different and can expire separately.

Method 2: The openssl command line

Use this for servers you can't easily open in a browser, for scripts, or for checking many hosts. It works on macOS, Linux and WSL on Windows:

`bash

echo | openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -servername example.com 2>/dev/null \

| openssl x509 -noout -enddate

`

The output looks like this:

`

notAfter=Mar 2 12:00:00 2027 GMT

`

Useful variations:

  • Show start and end dates together: replace -enddate with -dates.
  • Ask "does it expire within 30 days?": add -checkend 2592000 (30 days in seconds) to the x509 command. The exit code is 0 if the certificate is still valid at that point and 1 if it isn't, which makes this handy in cron jobs.
  • Check a mail server: use -connect mail.example.com:465, or -connect mail.example.com:587 -starttls smtp.
  • Check a certificate file on disk: openssl x509 -in cert.pem -noout -enddate.

On Windows PowerShell without openssl:

`powershell

$req = [Net.HttpWebRequest]::Create("https://example.com")

$req.GetResponse().Dispose()

$req.ServicePoint.Certificate.GetExpirationDateString()

`

Pitfalls:

  • Always pass -servername. Without it, a shared-hosting server may return its default certificate rather than your site's. This is a very common reason for a "wrong" date.
  • The time is in GMT. If a certificate expires early in the morning GMT, it can already be invalid during the evening before in the Americas.
  • Check www and the bare domain separately. They can be served by different certificates.

Method 3: An online checker

An online checker connects to the domain from outside your network and reports the expiry date, the issuer and whether the certificate chain is complete. Qualys SSL Labs' SSL Server Test gives a thorough report on each server. It's slow, but good for spotting a missing intermediate certificate, which can make some devices show warnings even when the expiry date is fine.

An online check is the best way to see what visitors see. It's also the only one of the three methods that doesn't depend on your own machine's settings or proxy.

What to do with the date

Once you have the expiry date:

  • More than 30 days left: nothing to do yet, but write the date down somewhere other than your head.
  • Under 30 days on an auto-renewing certificate (Let's Encrypt, cPanel AutoSSL, a host's free SSL): most auto-renewal tools try well before expiry. If the date isn't moving, renewal is probably failing. Typical causes are DNS pointing somewhere else, a blocked /.well-known/acme-challenge/ path, or a domain that was removed from the hosting account.
  • Under 30 days on a paid certificate: start the renewal now. Validation can take time, and you still have to install the new certificate.
  • Already expired: visitors see a "Your connection is not private" or "Not Secure" warning. Renew or reissue the certificate, install it, then re-check with Method 2 or 3 to confirm the new date is live. Your local browser may show a cached result.

When one site becomes twenty

The methods above are fine for a single domain. They get tedious once you look after a portfolio of client sites. Then the real question is "which of my sites expires next?", and checking each one by hand every few weeks is the kind of job that quietly stops getting done.

You can loop the openssl command over a list of domains (our guide on monitoring many sites has a ready-made script). Or you can use a monitoring service. We're building Lapsewatch for this case. It will show one list of every client domain sorted by days to expiry and send an email or Slack ping before any of them lapse. It's one option among several, and the free methods on this page work well if you only have a handful of sites.
